The site is every portable station's dream: a paved surface sixty by thirty metres, road
all the way up, public, no barrier in front of it, and the summit forty metres to the side.
Hainburg an der Donau below, Bratislava eleven kilometres east, the March and the Slovak
border in sight.

The question is not the site. The question is what rises above the horizon from there —
and whether Germany is part of it, where most of the other stations sit.

## How

The method is the same as for the [Feuerkogel](/en/blog/feuerkogel-horizont/#how),
[Stuhleck](/en/blog/stuhleck-horizont/) and the other sites in this series: SRTM terrain
model, one ray per degree out to 500 kilometres, ten metres of antenna height, earth
curvature with the 4/3 radius, obstacle names from Wikipedia. This time with the
**Austrian lidar survey** for the first 140 metres around the site — trees, knoll, hut —
and the contest logs laid over it.

Three classes: **clear** means more than three tenths of a degree below the horizontal,
**marginal** between −0.3° and zero, **blocked** means terrain above the horizontal.

## All the way round

![Polar chart of the radio horizon from the Braunsberg car park: a green arc to the south-east and east, continuous amber to the west and north-west, red areas up to almost three degrees to the south-west and north-east](../../../assets/karten/braunsberg-horizont-rundum-karte.png)

Of 360 degrees, **107 are clear, 113 marginal and 140 blocked**. That is the worst ratio
in the whole series — and still the site is not bad, because the clear degrees point where
the activity is.

Two hills close off half the circle. The **Hundsheimer Berg**, 480 metres high and three
kilometres south-west, stands in the way at up to **+2.76°** — it takes everything from
south through south-west to west: Graz, Slovenia, Croatia, Italy and the whole of the Alps.
The **Devínska Kobyla** on the Slovak side, 514 metres, five kilometres north-east, closes
the north-east at +1.84°: Kraków, Warsaw, eastern Poland.

And then there is the west. From 269° to 346° — the entire sector in which Germany lies —
the horizon stays between **−0.27° and −0.01°**. That is neither clear nor blocked: it is
the Vienna Woods, 34 to 99 kilometres out, sitting almost exactly on the line of sight.

## The site

![Site plan of the Braunsberg car park from the lidar survey with orthophoto: the paved surface, contour lines every two metres, the two mast positions and the six beam headings](../../../assets/karten/braunsberg-lageplan.png)

The lidar has nothing but good to say about the site itself. The asphalt lies at
**343 metres** (SRTM works with 337, the scan measures more precisely), it is level, and
within the first 140 metres almost nothing stands around it:

| Antenna height | Directions blocked by the near field |
|---|---|
| 6 m | 8° |
| 8 m | 3° |
| 10 m | none |
| 12 m and above | none |

With ten metres of mast the site is **free of its own near field in all 360 directions** —
no tree, no knoll, no building. The summit at 346 metres lies forty metres east and is
wooded, but it stands exactly where the Devínska Kobyla closes things off anyway. To the
west, where it would matter, the ground falls away as open meadow.

Two things that are not in the terrain model: the Braunsberg is a **Celtic oppidum** — a
registered archaeological site lies a hundred and seventy metres west of the car park, and
there are several memorials at the summit. Anyone putting masts into this ground, even with
pegs, clears that first. And the Hundsheimer Berge are a Natura 2000 area; the car park
itself lies at its edge.

## The stations

![Dark map 700 kilometres around Braunsberg, every contest station a dot: bright where the horizon is clear, amber behind terrain; plus the six rotator headings of the array](../../../assets/karten/braunsberg-stationen.png)

Of the 1 887 stations that appear in the logs within 700 kilometres, **1 172 are above the horizon** — Germany 454 of 480. That is the ceiling: no antenna, however large, gets past it.

The figure has two halves. **Genuinely clear** — horizon more than three tenths of a degree below the horizontal — are 341 stations, 0 of them in Germany. The other 831 are **marginal**: between −0.3° and zero, in the grazing shadow of an edge. Something works there, but at a loss of three to eight decibels depending on the day. Both numbers are given, because only both together describe the site. Of the 107 degrees that are clear, 113 marginal degrees come on top.

## The antennas

![Sheet “The antenna array”: polar chart of the stations within 500 kilometres of Braunsberg, stacked by country in ten-degree bins, with the three headings of the DL stack and the three of the rotator stack](../../../assets/karten/braunsberg-anlage.png)

The same array is computed everywhere as the one on the Stuhleck, with **three masts**:

- **Mast 2**, 7 metres, rotator: two 12JXX2 stacked at 3.9 and 6.7 m — the stack that looks at Germany.
- **Mast 1**, 10 metres, rotator: two 12JXX2 stacked at 6.9 and 9.7 m, 34° wide, 17.8 dBi.
- **Mast 3**, 7 metres, no rotator: two stacked 9-element Tonnas at 3.9 and 6.7 m, 44° wide, 14.3 dBi — fixed on one heading.

The headings are not guessed but searched: for Braunsberg they come out at **308° / 280° / 326°** for the DL stack, **4° / 156° / 106°** for the rotator stack and **168°** for the fixed Tonnas.

Feeding goes through a switch. With the full 1 000 watts on whichever system is being worked, the array reaches **1 124 stations** (Germany 443, by the DARC list 814). Split across the two rotator stacks, 500 watts each, it is 900; spread over all three systems at once only 757 — three directions cost more power than they gain in coverage. The Tonna mast alone adds 20 stations that would otherwise be missing.

## The comparison

![Bar chart of every site in the series with the same array: one bar per site of the stations it reaches, stacked by country](../../../assets/karten/standorte-vergleich-serie.png)

Every site in this series with **the same array, the same counting rule and the same logs** — only then are the numbers comparable. Reached means: horizon below the horizontal and enough gain for the distance (6 dBi to 300 km, then 3 dB per further 100 km), with 1 000 watts on whichever system is being worked.

The count runs on the **IARU logs 2024/2025 and the Marconi 2025**: they record every country alike. The DARC list covers Germany only — it sits in its own column, otherwise every site that looks west wins on the data alone.

| Loser · 1 585 m | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | drive to the top |

Braunsberg therefore comes **5 of 8**.

Anyone who knows the earlier articles in this series will find different numbers there: those were computed with the first line-up — one Yagi stack and one quad stack with a 69° beamwidth. Since the choice fell on two narrow 12JXX2 stacks, the order shifts: more gain and less width favours the sites whose stations bunch in one direction, and costs the ones that stand open all round. The fixed Tonnas on mast 3 win part of that width back.

## What it means

For the east and south-east the Braunsberg is a very good site, and with a convenience no
mountain site offers: drive up, set up, done. **Bratislava −0.40°, Budapest −0.33°,
Timișoara −0.42°, Belgrade −0.30°, Bucharest −0.32°, Brno −0.30°, Wrocław −0.31°** — all
clear, most of it over flat land. There is enough activity in the Little Hungarian Plain
and along the Danube during a contest, and the distances count double.

Towards Germany it is the weakest site in the series, and not by a little. **Not one of
the thirty German cities stands genuinely clear:** Munich −0.05°, Passau −0.02°, Stuttgart
−0.07°, Nuremberg −0.12°, Frankfurt −0.09°, Leipzig −0.13°, Dresden −0.20°, Hamburg −0.21°,
Berlin −0.24°, Cologne −0.23°. All of them between zero and a quarter of a degree below —
the Vienna Woods clip every one of those paths. That does not mean nothing works: an edge
at fifty kilometres costs three to eight decibels depending on the day, and with 1 000 watts
on one stack 443 German stations are in the calculation (814 by the DARC list) that would be reachable elsewhere. But they never arrive cleanly, and a tropo evening helps less here than on a
mountain that stands free.

**Mast height changes nothing about this.** Five metres more antenna shifts the angle to an
edge fifty kilometres away by six thousandths of a degree. What is missing here is not
metres but kilometres of distance from the Vienna Woods — or a position behind them.

So anyone running a contest from the Braunsberg turns east and south-east and takes Germany
for what it is there: a sector you work when conditions play along, not the one you point
the fixed antenna at. For Germany, [Grünberg](/en/blog/gruenberg-horizont/),
[Gaisberg](/en/blog/gaisberg-horizont/) and [Feuerkogel](/en/blog/feuerkogel-horizont/)
remain the addresses.

## Caveat

A calculation, not a measurement. The SRTM grid is thirty metres, the lidar four; the far
field knows neither trees nor buildings, only terrain. Propagation assumes a standard
atmosphere — a tropo evening computes differently, and in the marginal directions it decides
more than any tenth of a degree in this table. The station count is a model calculation with
antenna patterns from manufacturer data, not a prediction of contacts.

What the calculation does say for certain: where this site stands open, and that the west
has an edge.

The rest gets measured — up there, with an antenna.
